Part I — The email

Write the email people actually reply to

The same study found that the top 10% of reps book 8.1× more meetings than the average rep — 23 meetings to the average rep's 3. They don't work 8× harder; they get 2.1× the opens and 4.2× the replies by writing differently. Here's what they do.

Lesson 01The email

Outbound is a skill, not a volume game

Two reps fail in opposite ways: one blasts 1,000 emails a week and books nothing, the other spends hours over-researching a single message and still gets ignored. The winners sit in the middle — a repeatable message, sent to the right accounts, enough times. Everything below is about that message and that rhythm.

Top reps: 23 meetings Average rep: 3 meetings = 8.1× more
Takeaway: Don't choose between volume and quality — build one good message you can send at scale.
★
At Mozaik: A contract rep working a shortlist of 40 target hotels with a sharp message beats one spraying 400 random addresses.
Lesson 02The email

The subject line decides if you're even opened

Across millions of emails the pattern was blunt: short, lowercase subject lines that read like an internal note win. Keep it under four words, lowercase, about their world — not a marketing headline. Buzzwords, numbers and name-dropping all lowered open rates, and an empty subject line reads as spam (it cut replies by about 12%).

Under 4 words lowercase a priority, not a pitch
Takeaway: Write the subject like a colleague would — "lobby refit", "banquet seating", "showroom lighting".
★
At Mozaik: "new restaurant fit-out" will beat "Premium Turkish Furniture — 20% Off for Hospitality Partners" every time.
Lesson 03The email

Under 100 words, four sentences, no scrolling

Reply rates climbed as emails got shorter — the sweet spot was under 100 words and three to four sentences, short enough to read on a phone without scrolling. The shape that works: one line of personalization → the problem → a one-sentence solution → the ask. Everything else is deleted before it's read.

Takeaway: If your email needs a scroll on mobile, it's too long. Cut it to four sentences.
★
At Mozaik: Save the full catalogue, lead times and finishes for the reply — the first email just earns the reply.
Lesson 04The email

Speak their language, not ours

Top reps used far more "you / your / your team" and far less "we / our / I". Pronoun-heavy, prospect-focused emails read as personal even when they're automated, while pitch words and seller jargon measurably dropped reply rates. The email should be about their problem, not our product.

Takeaway: Count your "you"s versus your "we"s. If "we" wins, rewrite it.
★
At Mozaik: "your 120 rooms need to feel consistent on a tight opening date" beats "we are a leading manufacturer with 40 years of craftsmanship".
Lesson 05The email

Personalize on the problem, not a fun fact

Personalization did lift replies and meetings — but not all personalization is equal. Referencing someone's university or a hobby barely moved the needle. What worked was a high-intent signal or a real business problem — a project they just announced, a location they're opening, a role they're hiring. Prioritise the high-intent accounts first, then speak to their problem.

Takeaway: Open with something they're working on this quarter, not something from their LinkedIn "about" section.
★
At Mozaik: "saw the planning notice for your second branch in Nişantaşı" is a reason to reply; "fellow Galatasaray fan" is not.
Lesson 06The email

Make an offer — don't beg for a meeting

The weakest ending is "do you have 30 minutes next week?" Asking for a meeting puts all the cost on them and gives nothing back. The strongest CTAs ask for interest ("open to seeing what this would look like?") or make an offer — something useful they get even if they never buy: a quote, a sample, a moodboard, a showroom visit.

Takeaway: End with "open to…?" or a low-friction offer, not a calendar demand.
★
At Mozaik: "want me to put together a quick furniture + lighting moodboard for the lobby?" gives value before the first call.
02
Part II — The sequence

One email is not outreach — run a sequence

A single email almost never breaks into a good account. The sequence template built on this data runs 10–14 touches over about 30 days, across email, phone and LinkedIn, grouped into themes. Spreading touches across channels roughly doubles email reply rates by drawing attention back to the inbox.

Lesson 07The sequence

10–14 touches over 30 days, across channels

Breaking into an account takes persistence and multiple angles. The reference sequence is roughly 7 emails, 4 calls and 3 LinkedIn touches over 30 days — about six emails in the first 14–28 days is where replies peak, after which they fall below 0.5% and it's time to rest the account. Never send one email and give up.

Takeaway: Plan a 30-day sequence per account, not a one-off email.
★
At Mozaik: A €200k fit-out is worth a month of thoughtful touches across email, phone and LinkedIn.
Lesson 08The sequence

Sell one thing at a time

Don't cram every product and benefit into one email — long "everything" emails get deleted. Lead with the single biggest problem to draw the first replies, and if that doesn't land, change the subject line and move to the next biggest problem. Each theme is a fresh angle with a fresh subject line, not a repeat of the same pitch.

Takeaway: One problem per theme. New theme, new subject line.
★
At Mozaik: Theme 1: consistency across 120 rooms. Theme 2: hitting the opening date. Theme 3: one supplier for furniture + lighting.
Lesson 09The sequence

Bump emails: one sentence, reply in the thread

A "bump" resurfaces your best email by replying to it in the same thread. Keep it to one sentence — a simple "any thoughts?" bump often gets as many replies as the original. The data was clear: short bumps beat long ones, and switching from more pitching to "thoughts?" / a rephrased ask (and eventually a break-up) nearly doubled reply rates.

Takeaway: Bump with one line in the existing thread — never re-pitch the whole thing.
★
At Mozaik: "Just left you a voicemail — worth a quick look?" tied to the first email does the job.
Lesson 10The sequence

The break-up: ask for a yes or a no

When nothing lands, stop pitching and give them an easy way out — a short note that asks for a straight yes or no, or offers to stop reaching out. Break-up language nearly doubled reply rates in the study, because it nudges the people on the fence who keep meaning to reply. Keep it professional: ask "still worth a conversation, or should I close this out?", not a gimmicky "1-2-3" message.

Takeaway: End the sequence by asking permission to stop — it pulls out the last replies.
★
At Mozaik: "I'll assume the timing isn't right and close this off — unless you'd like a quote before you decide?"
03
Part III — The other channels

Use LinkedIn and the phone to lift your email

LinkedIn and cold calls rarely close the deal on their own in outbound — their real job is to drive attention back to your email, where the conversation actually happens. Use them to put a face and a voice to your name, then point back to the inbox.

Lesson 11The other channels

LinkedIn's job is to raise your email replies

Don't pitch in the DM. The pattern that works: a blank connection request (personalised requests trigger "sales alarms" and get accepted less), then a short context-only message that points back to the email, then a one-line bump, and optionally a short video. Each LinkedIn touch mirrors an email touch and quietly lifts your reply rate.

Takeaway: Connect blank, reference the email, don't sell in the DM.
★
At Mozaik: "sent you a note about the lobby project — did it reach you?" is enough; keep the catalogue in the email.
Lesson 12The other channels

Cold calls and voicemails multiply email replies

Adding calls to a sequence doubled to tripled email reply rates in the data. You don't need to close on the call — a short voicemail that says "I'm about to email you, no need to call back, just reply and tell me if it's even slightly interesting" sends people straight to the inbox. The phone makes the email feel real.

Takeaway: Leave a short VM that points to the email, then send the email right after.
★
At Mozaik: A 20-second voicemail before your email turns a cold name into a warm reply far more often.

The one thing to remember

A short, human email about their problem — sent enough times, across enough channels.

You don't need to be clever or work twice as hard. Write four sentences in their language about one real problem, make an easy offer, and give the account a month of thoughtful touches. That's how the top reps book 8× the meetings.
