The science of a happier vacation.

Nine rules from the happiness research on choosing the right kind of break, staying present while you're on it, and coming back without the crash. Rest is not the opposite of performance — it's part of the scaffolding.

Rule 01Before You Go

Know your motive before you book

Six motives show up again and again in the research: exploration, escape, people, nature, history and prestige. Most disappointing holidays are simply a mismatch — an escape motive spent on a punishing itinerary, or a nature motive spent in a resort lobby. Name your top two or three first, then design the trip around them. Prestige is the one motive that reliably backfires, because it runs on social comparison rather than on anything you'll actually enjoy.

Takeaway: Pick the trip that fits your motive, not the one that photographs well. Incongruence — not budget — ruins most vacations. And whatever your motive, sharing it with people you love amplifies it.
★
At Mozaik: Ask the team what they actually want from their leave before you build the summer rota. The person who needs quiet and the person who wants a city break don't need the same two weeks in August.
Rule 02Before You Go

Savor the anticipation — it's part of the trip

Studies of Dutch and Chinese travellers find that people with a trip coming are happier than people without one — before they have gone anywhere. That's dopamine doing what it actually does: not pleasure, but anticipation of reward, the wanting-learning-liking loop firing while the reward is still on its way. Booking early and reading about where you're going extends the vacation backwards by weeks.

Takeaway: Plan the itinerary in advance and enjoy studying it. Weeks of looking forward are a real part of the return on a trip — a last-minute booking simply throws them away.
★
At Mozaik: Lock leave dates early in the year. The team gets months of anticipation for free, and Operations gets a coverage plan instead of a surprise.
Rule 03Before You Go

Manage your expectations about the glow

A 2010 Dutch study found vacationers were healthier, less stressed, more energetic and in better spirits while away — and that most of the effect was gone by the end of the first week back. That isn't a failed vacation, it's how the limbic system works. A permanently elevated mood would stop you noticing threats and opportunities, so your brain refuses to hand one out.

Takeaway: A good trip buys real recovery, great memories and a short afterglow. It won't fix the job you return to — expecting that is what makes people feel cheated by a perfectly good holiday.
Rule 04Before You Go

Take vacationettes, not one epic

If the glow fades within a week, frequency beats duration. Fifteen long weekends nearby hand you fifteen anticipations and fifteen afterglows; one month far away hands you one of each. Unless the distance itself is the point, split most of your allowance into short, repeatable breaks.

Takeaway: Same days off, more doses. Keep one longer trip if you want the distance — spend the rest in small, frequent pieces.
★
At Mozaik: Short breaks are also far easier to cover. A Friday-to-Monday rarely leaves a showroom short or a Kurumsal project unattended the way a three-week absence does.
Rule 05While You're There

Take fewer pictures

The prefrontal cortex lets us live in three time zones at once — and we are far better at the past and the future than at the present. A photo is a small act of time travel: you step out of the moment in order to store it for a self who will look back on it. A few pictures, fine. A trip experienced through a screen is a trip you mostly didn't attend.

Takeaway: Shoot a few, then put the phone away. The point isn't to document the vacation — it's to be on it.
Rule 06While You're There

Don't post about it

Posting is usually the prestige motive in disguise: the aim is envy, and envy runs on social comparison. Evolution wired you for status, not for happiness — which is why the urge feels so natural, and why acting on it puts your vacation in someone else's hands. You end up living the trip for an audience that isn't there.

Takeaway: If you're curating the trip for viewers, you're working, not resting. Save the photos for the people who were with you.
Rule 07While You're There

Don't take work with you

Research in Stress and Health finds that engaging in work-related activity while away lowers the positive effects of the vacation 1, 3 and 10 days after returning. Workaholics take a little work along so the inbox doesn't pile up; what they actually do is delete the recovery they went for. If you need occupation after the kids are asleep, read something nobody is paying you to learn.

Takeaway: No email, no “just one call”. Working on vacation is the fastest way to lose the entire benefit of it — and the surest way to annoy everyone you brought along.
★
At Mozaik: Hand over properly before you go: named cover, open items listed, out-of-office on. A colleague empowered to decide in your absence is worth more than your laptop on the beach.
Rule 08Coming Back

Come home early

Easing back beats travelling until the last possible hour. Researchers find that people who leave themselves a buffer enjoy the whole break more. Land on Friday instead of Sunday night and you get two days at home that work like a second small holiday — plus something to look forward to at the tail end of the first.

Takeaway: Build one or two buffer days in before your first day back. Unpacking at midnight on Sunday is how a good trip turns into a bad Monday.
★
At Mozaik: Put the buffer day inside the leave request, not outside it. A rep who walks in rested on Monday is worth more than one extra day away.
Rule 09Coming Back

Brace for re-entry

The contrast that makes a vacation feel wonderful runs in reverse on the way home: ordinary life after a great week is a carrot after a piece of candy. Expect the dip instead of being ambushed by it, and don't read it as a verdict on your job, your city or your life. A useful policy: make no significant decisions in your first week back.

Takeaway: Name the feeling as contrast, not truth. Knowing what's happening is the difference between managing your feelings and being managed by them.
★
At Mozaik: Extend the same grace to others. The first week back is for catching up — not for launching an initiative or judging someone's performance.
The one thing to remember

Rest is not time away from the work. It is part of the work.

A break chosen for the right motive, lived without a screen between you and it, and ended a day early is worth more than twice as long spent half at the office. Protect it the way you'd protect a deadline.
