Part I — Team or Meeting?

Teams don't happen by accident

Most of us work in groups we call teams. Pope's uncomfortable question is whether we really are one — or just a recurring meeting of busy individuals.

Concept 01The Premise

A team is a decision, not a default

Most workplaces quietly assume that if you put capable people in the same department, a team will form by itself. Pope argues the opposite: cohesion has to be chosen and worked at. The irony is that the busiest groups feel they have the least time for it — yet much of their busyness is caused by poor teamwork: workarounds, the difficult conversation nobody starts, no shared view on what matters most. Left alone, most groups settle into meeting mode: people report in, coordinate when they have to, and only act as one when a crisis forces them to.

Takeaway: If your group only feels like a team during a fire, you're a meeting that occasionally becomes a team.
★
At Mozaik: The week a big hotel order is about to slip, the showroom, Kurumsal, the depo and accounts suddenly talk every hour. The real question is why it takes a crisis to work like that.
Concept 02The Weather

Teamwork happens between the meetings — and moods spread

Teamwork isn't the weekly meeting. It's the hundred small moments around it: a handover at the showroom desk, a reply in the group chat, how a delay gets announced. It's as much a mindset as a process — and mindsets are contagious. People unconsciously copy the mood of those around them, so one permanently gloomy voice (Pope's example is Eeyore from Winnie-the-Pooh) can pull a whole team into “nothing ever works here”. Realism is healthy; letting overload, helplessness and cynicism spread unchecked is not. Keeping the mood honest but constructive is everyone's job, not just the manager's.

Takeaway: You are always setting the weather for someone. Choose it on purpose.
★
At Mozaik: The first person on the showroom floor in the morning sets the tone for everyone who arrives after — and so does the first message in the team group.

Part II — The Five Mindsets

How high-performing teams think

Five shared beliefs set high-performing teams apart. Each is simple to say and hard to live — and each one has an everyday test.

Concept 03Mindset 1

We play to win

A team that plays to win is ambitious: it takes calculated risks, goes after new opportunities and bounces back from setbacks without losing heart. Its opposite is playing not to lose — protecting the present, avoiding anything that could go wrong, preferring safety to bigger rewards. Two things make playing to win possible. First, a clear definition of winning: in sport the scoreboard does it for you, but at work someone has to decide what a win is, or teams default to simply not losing. Second, a home advantage: a team where “I'm stuck, can you help?” is welcomed rather than judged, so people play boldly instead of guarding every flank.

Takeaway: Ask your team what a win looks like this quarter. If the answers don't match, you're probably playing not to lose.
★
At Mozaik: “Not losing” is matching last year's numbers. “Winning” might be: every architect who visits the showroom leaves with a follow-up meeting in the diary. Name it, so everyone knows what they're playing for.
Concept 04Mindset 2

We before me

No individual is bigger than the team's mission. In a we-before-me team people make sacrifices in their own area for the good of the whole. Without it, even brilliant individuals can deliver short-term results while their personal goals slowly drift away from the team's — until the team is just a loose set of projects and personal launch-pads. Pope describes the ideal member as T-shaped: deep expertise in your own area (the vertical bar) plus a broad view of the whole organisation (the horizontal bar), which is what makes working across functions possible. It also demands humility: no task is beneath anyone. He cites the New Zealand All Blacks, where players clean their own changing room after games.

Takeaway: Is anyone in your team “too senior” to carry a box, answer the phone or tidy the samples? That's where “me” is winning.
★
At Mozaik: The Kurumsal closer who walks a retail customer through the lighting range because the Aydınlatma colleague is with another client — that's the horizontal bar of the T at work.
Concept 05Mindset 3

We do what we say we will do

Of course everyone keeps their word — in theory. In practice small missed commitments pile up: arriving late, half-listening in a meeting, agreeing to something and never doing it. Each one quietly says that promises here are optional. Pope describes a team that, for three and a half weeks every month, confidently told its manager the forecast would be met — then missed it in the last days, with a new reason every time. It was running on hope instead of reality. In high-performing teams people keep promises to each other, not just to the boss, and hold each other to account in a supportive way: “How can we help? What do you need?”

Takeaway: The cheapest way to build trust is to make smaller promises and keep every one of them.
★
At Mozaik: “I'll send the quote tomorrow” is a promise to the customer and to the colleague who already told the customer it's coming. Missing it lets both of them down.
Concept 06Mindset 4

We talk to, not about, each other

Gossip is easy to slip into and quietly corrosive: corridor complaints, eye-rolls, a passive-aggressive emoji. Talking to someone rather than about them is one of the foundations of trust. A common trap is the hub-and-spoke team, where every complaint travels to the manager and colleagues rarely solve anything with each other. The manager becomes a referee and messy issues bounce around for weeks. In high-performing teams people raise issues with each other first, argue it out in private, and then present a united front — even when the agreed route wasn't everyone's first choice.

Takeaway: Before you take a problem about a colleague to the manager, take it to the colleague.
★
At Mozaik: If delivery dates keep coming back wrong, the showroom talks to the depo — not about the depo at lunch, and not straight to management.
Concept 07Mindset 5

We learn, we adapt, we improve

The fifth mindset is the confidence to turn failures into team learning and the stamina to change plans fast. In many teams learning only happens at formal trainings, and failure is something to pin on a person or the market and quietly sweep away. But innovation and failure come as a pair. The real enemy is shame: failure says “that approach didn't work”, shame says “I am the problem” — and shame makes people play not to lose. Pope tells the story of James Barker at Finastra, who created awards for well-reasoned ideas that didn't work out, and for people who got rid of pointless rules, to make experimenting feel safe.

Takeaway: Separate the decision from the person. Ask “what did we learn?” before “whose fault was it?”
★
At Mozaik: A Mozaik version: once a month, each team names one rule we should stop following — the form nobody reads, the approval nobody needs.
03
Part III — The Framework

Four areas every team has to look after

Mindsets only stick when systems and habits back them up. Pope's framework puts purpose at the centre and three areas around it.

Concept 08The Map

Purpose, inner game, outer game, wellbeing

Mindsets need systems and habits behind them to last. Pope's high-performing teams framework has four areas. Purpose sits at the centre and connects the other two: the inner game — how we work together inside the team — and the outer game — how we work with the world outside it. Around all three sits wellbeing: the energy that keeps the whole thing running over time. A team needs all four; strength in one rarely makes up for neglect in another.

Takeaway: Most teams obsess over one area — usually the inner game — and then wonder why the rest wobbles.
★
At Mozaik: Showroom teams often live in the inner game (rotas, floor rules); Kurumsal often lives in the outer game (clients, tenders). Both need all four.
Concept 09Area 1

Purpose: the value only we can create together

Team purpose is the heart of the framework. It gives everyone clarity about the unique value the team creates together — things none of its members could achieve alone. It works as an anchor when the team faces complicated decisions, and as the glue that holds people together when the pressure is on. Three questions test it: what can only we create together, what do we want to be known for, and why does this work matter to each of us personally?

Takeaway: If you can't say your team's purpose in one sentence, decisions will be made by whoever argues loudest.
★
At Mozaik: A first draft for a showroom team: “We make a client's space feel finished — from the first sketch to the last lamp switched on.” Argue with it, then make it yours.
Concept 10Area 2

The inner game: who, what and how

The idea of an inner and an outer game comes from Timothy Gallwey's 1970s book The Inner Game of Tennis, which separated the mental side of performance from the physical one. For a team, the inner game covers three things: the right membership (the who), the unique work of the team (the what), and how members behave towards each other (the how). One warning: left unchecked, the inner game can swallow a team's energy. Many teams look only inward — and forget that the job is out there.

Takeaway: Make the “how” explicit: three behaviours you expect from each other, written down and agreed.
★
At Mozaik: For example: we reply to each other within the working day; nobody hears bad news last; client handovers happen in writing.
Concept 11Area 3

The outer game: value, insight and one story

Reporting in on your own work isn't enough. High-performing teams also face outwards. The outer game covers three things: creating value for the team's stakeholders, harnessing external insights and trends, and presenting one clear, consistent story about the team to the outside world. Teams that do this well regularly ask stakeholders how they could deliver more, and bring outside perspectives in while pushing their own mission forward.

Takeaway: Agree the message before you leave the room. Five versions of the truth outside is a team problem, not a communication problem.
★
At Mozaik: An architect, a hotel group and a supplier in Italy should hear the same delivery date from whoever they happen to speak to at Mozaik.
Concept 12Area 4

Wellbeing: spend energy, and renew it

What looks like a teamwork problem is sometimes simply exhaustion. Focus turns into fatigue, and collaboration into a sense of burnout. This part of the framework goes beyond the usual wellbeing perks: it looks at the root causes of overload — how the team prioritises, how it handles trade-offs, and how it manages people joining and leaving. The goal is a team that knows not only how to spend its energy, but how to renew it.

Takeaway: The question isn't “are we working hard?” It's “can we keep this up?”
★
At Mozaik: After a big project install or the year-end rush, plan the recovery as carefully as the delivery.

What the score means

30–60
Just starting out — or stuck

Right now this feels more like a group of individuals sharing a calendar than a team. People mostly look after their own tasks, the shared commitment is thin, and meetings drift into routine. It needs clear direction and a sense that everyone owns the result. Start with purpose: agree, in one sentence, what only this team can create together.

61–90
Strong in a crisis, uneven the rest of the time

When something is urgent the team pulls together and performs. In ordinary weeks decisions stall, sub-groups form and too much effort goes into working around the system — a fast route to burnout. Revisit the purpose and be brave about dropping work that doesn't serve it.

91–120
Steady, with room to grow

Trust has been earned through hard moments; people know each other's strengths and the team looks outward. The risk now is comfort: conversations that recycle familiar ideas. Push deliberately for fresh thinking and new information.

121–150
Built for high performance

Equal attention inside and outside the team, a reputation for delivering, honest debate and full commitment once a decision is made. Keep the edge by making sure newcomers are brought in to contribute, not just to fit in — and that people leave well.
