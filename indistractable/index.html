Distraction isn't a technology problem. It's a discomfort problem.

Fourteen ideas from Nir Eyal's Indistractable, redrawn for the Mozaik team — why we really reach for the phone, how to plan a week nobody can steal, and how to make the inbox, the group chat and the meeting room work for us instead of the other way round.

01
Part I — Master Internal Triggers

Why we really reach for the phone

Distraction doesn't start on the screen. It starts a second earlier, inside, with a feeling we'd rather not sit in. Everything else in this training depends on getting that order right.

Concept 01The Model

The opposite of distraction isn't focus — it's traction

Both words come from the same Latin root, trahere, to pull. Traction pulls you toward what you planned; distraction pulls you away from it. Which one an action is has nothing to do with how it looks from outside and everything to do with whether you meant to do it. And every action starts with a trigger — either external (a ping, a colleague at your desk, a screen) or internal (a feeling).

Takeaway: Stop asking “was I busy today?” Ask “did I do what I said I'd do?” Everything else was distraction, however useful it looked.
★
At Mozaik: Answering a customer's WhatsApp is traction if that hour was for customers, and distraction if that hour was for building the Kurumsal quote. Same act — the calendar decides which.
Concept 02The Cause

Time management is pain management

We don't check the phone because the phone is interesting. We check it because something inside is uncomfortable — boredom, uncertainty, fatigue, loneliness, anxiety, the fear of missing something. The phone is the exit, not the cause. Which is why taking it away rarely works: the discomfort simply finds another door — the fridge, the news, a sudden and urgent need to tidy the desk.

Takeaway: When you catch yourself drifting, don't ask what distracted you. Ask what you were trying to get away from — the answer is usually one word long.
★
At Mozaik: The hour before a difficult price conversation is the hour the inbox looks most interesting. That's not coincidence. That's the tell.
Concept 03The Technique

You can't stop the urge. You can outlast it.

Urges behave like waves: they rise, crest and fall, usually inside a couple of minutes. Fighting one makes it louder; watching it makes it quieter. Two moves. First, write the feeling down the moment it arrives — “restless, 15:40, halfway through the quote”. Second, the ten-minute rule: you may have the distraction, in ten minutes. Most of the time the wave has broken before the ten minutes are up. In clinical trials, teaching smokers to notice and ride out a craving rather than suppress it roughly doubled quit rates.

Takeaway: Watch the liminal moments — the seconds between finishing one thing and starting the next. That gap is where “just a quick look” lives. Say the next task out loud before you pick anything up.
★
At Mozaik: Between two showroom appointments, the ten minutes belong to the next customer's file, not to the feed. Name it in the doorway and it usually holds.
Concept 04The Reframe

Make the boring part a game — and stop calling yourself distractible

Two reframes do most of the work. The task: the cure for boredom isn't an easier job, it's a harder one — hunt for the novelty inside the thing you're avoiding. How fast can this be done cleanly? How few revisions? What pattern is hiding in this list you've read forty times? Yourself: people who believe willpower is a tank that empties run out sooner than people who don't. Willpower behaves far more like an emotion — it comes and goes — than like fuel. And self-talk is a prediction, not a diagnosis: “I'm rubbish at concentrating” is a sentence that makes itself true.

Takeaway: Talk to yourself the way you'd talk to a colleague who just lost an hour: kindly first, practically second. Self-criticism makes the next hour worse, not better.
★
At Mozaik: Stock reconciliation and data entry are the obvious candidates: set a time to beat, or a zero-error streak to keep. The work doesn't get shorter — it gets playable.
02
Part II — Make Time for Traction

A week you decided on in advance

You cannot defend a plan you never made. This part turns what you say matters into blocks in a calendar — including the blocks for doing nothing.

Concept 05Values into Time

You can't call it a distraction unless you know what it pulled you away from

A to-do list is a wish list; a calendar is a decision. Eyal's rule is to schedule inputs, not outcomes — you can't promise that the deal closes, you can promise the two hours. And a week has to cover three domains, not one: you, your relationships, and your work. Values aren't goals you finish and tick off — they're attributes of the person you want to be, and they only exist where they show up in the week.

Takeaway: An empty calendar isn't freedom. It's an open invitation for someone else to decide what your day was for.
★
At Mozaik: If “I want to be present at home” is a value, it is a block — not a hope you get to after the last email. Same for the hour of pipeline work that never survives a busy showroom day.
Concept 06The Method

Timebox the week, then fix it every Friday

Timeboxing is an implementation intention: deciding in advance what you'll do and when. A timeboxed week isn't a promise to be productive every minute — it's a decision, made while you're calm, about what each hour is for. Including the empty ones: time you planned to waste is not wasted time. Then the part everyone skips — fifteen minutes at the end of the week with two questions: when did I do something other than what I planned? and what one change will I make next week? The schedule is a hypothesis you're testing, not a contract you're failing.

Takeaway: Judge the week on whether you did what the box said, not on how much you got done. Output is a hope; input is a promise you can actually keep.
★
At Mozaik: Friday, fifteen minutes, before you leave. Two questions, one change. It is the cheapest process improvement available to any of us.
Concept 07Schedule Syncing

Show your calendar to the people who can wreck it

Most “always-on” pressure is assumed rather than stated. Nobody said the quote had to be answered at 22:40; we inferred it, and then resented it. The fix is a conversation with the calendar open, not a complaint: here's how I've planned the week; if this new thing comes first, tell me what comes out. That turns an argument about effort — which nobody wins — into a conversation about priorities, which is a manager's actual job. The same sentence works at home, where the unspoken expectations are usually louder.

Takeaway: The sentence that changes everything: “This is what I planned. If something new comes first, tell me what comes out.”
★
At Mozaik: Worth doing between showroom and operations especially — the rota and the deadline are usually planned by different people who've never seen each other's week.
03
Part III — Hack Back External Triggers

Pings, inboxes and the meeting that could have been a message

Now — and only now — the settings screen. Not to go off-grid, but to make sure every interruption that reaches you is one you'd have chosen.

Concept 08The Audit

Every trigger gets one question: does this serve me, or do I serve it?

External triggers aren't the enemy — the wrong ones are. A customer calling back is a trigger that serves you. A red badge you never asked for, on an app you opened twice, is one you serve. Four moves, once, properly: remove what you don't need, replace what you do need with a better time and place for it, rearrange so the first screen holds only tools you chose deliberately, and reclaim the notification settings — most people have never opened that screen in their life.

Takeaway: Do the audit once, with the phone in your hand and the settings screen open. Twenty minutes, and it buys back months.
★
At Mozaik: One rule worth agreeing as a team: nothing that isn't a person expecting an answer gets to make a sound during showroom hours.
Concept 09Inbox & Chat

To get fewer emails, send fewer emails

Every message you send is a request for a reply, so the volume you receive is partly a bill you wrote yourself. Then two habits. Delay and batch: decide when you answer, not whether — and say so, because a line at the top of a message (“no rush, Thursday is fine”) costs you five seconds and gives the other person their afternoon back. Group chat is a sauna, not a shower: short, scheduled, everyone in at once, then out. It is a terrible place to think out loud, because your thinking becomes everyone else's interruption.

Takeaway: Nobody is entitled to your attention the second they type. A reply window is a courtesy you announce, not a failure you apologise for.
★
At Mozaik: Worth naming out loud: which of our WhatsApp groups are “answer today” and which are “answer now”. Right now every group is treated as the second, which is why none of them work.
Concept 10Meetings

No agenda, no meeting

A meeting that exists to work out what we think is the most expensive way to think. Six people for an hour is a working day, spent. So: an agenda and a short written brief, circulated first — and notice how often writing the brief dissolves the need for the meeting altogether. Meetings are for building consensus around a proposal, not for generating one. Devices stay outside; one laptop for notes, on the screen where everyone can see it.

Takeaway: If you can't write down what the meeting decides, you don't have a meeting. You have a conversation that could have been a message.
★
At Mozaik: Try it on the weekly: one page, sent the evening before, three decisions listed. The meeting usually gets shorter by half and better by more than that.
04
Part IV — Prevent Distraction with Pacts

The last line of defence

A pact is a decision made by today's you that tomorrow's you can't easily undo. It is the final step, not the first one.

Concept 11Precommitment

Make the wrong thing harder than the right thing

Pacts are the last step for a reason: lock the door before you've dealt with the other three and you've simply locked yourself in with the discomfort. Once the groundwork is done, two kinds work. Effort pacts add friction — the app off the phone, the phone in a drawer in another room, a site blocker that runs during the deep-work box. Five seconds of friction beats five minutes of willpower. Price pacts add a cost: money on the table if the thing doesn't happen. They work, and they are brutal if you picked the wrong goal — so use them on inputs you control, never on outcomes you don't.

Takeaway: Put a small cost between you and the thing you'll regret. Design the friction while you're calm; you will not invent it in the moment.
★
At Mozaik: The simplest version, and the one customers notice: the phone goes face-down in the drawer for the length of an appointment.
Concept 12Identity

The strongest pact is the one you make with who you are

We keep promises that match our self-image far more reliably than promises that fight it. A vegetarian doesn't resist the steak — they don't consider it. So say it: “I'm indistractable.” Out loud, to colleagues, in the words you use about yourself. It sounds like a slogan and it is actually the mechanism: it moves the question from “why shouldn't I check?” to “that's not what I do.” Rituals help. Telling other people helps most, because now breaking it costs something.

Takeaway: Stop saying “I'm trying to focus more.” Say “I don't check my phone in meetings.” Then be seen not doing it.
★
At Mozaik: Identity spreads faster than instruction. One person who visibly doesn't reach for the phone mid-conversation changes a showroom faster than any policy.
05
Part V — Bigger Than You

The team, and the honest counterargument

Two things the model can't do on its own: fix a culture where nobody feels safe saying “this doesn't work”, and win an argument with an industry built to interrupt you.

Concept 13The Workplace

Constant interruption is a symptom of culture, not of software

When a whole team is always on, the tools get blamed — but the cause is usually that nobody feels safe saying “this doesn't work”. Leslie Perlow's experiment at Boston Consulting Group gave consultants one predictable night off a week; the striking result wasn't the night off, it was that the weekly conversation about how to protect it was what actually changed the culture. That conversation needs psychological safety — Amy Edmondson's term for the shared belief that you won't be punished or humiliated for speaking up. Her three moves for whoever is leading: frame the work as a learning problem, admit your own fallibility, and model curiosity by asking far more questions than you answer.

Takeaway: If everyone's distracted, don't buy a tool. Book the conversation where people can say what's actually broken — and make sure the most senior person in the room speaks last.
★
At Mozaik: Twenty minutes of the monthly meeting is enough to start: what interrupts you most, what you assume is expected of you after hours, and which of those assumptions is actually true.
Concept 14The Other Side

The honest counterargument

Take this book with a hand on the other side of the scale. Nir Eyal wrote Hooked — the manual on building habit-forming products — before he wrote this one, which is an unusual CV for an author arguing the design isn't the problem. Johann Hari's Stolen Focus makes the opposite case: attention is being taken at industrial scale by companies with more resources than any of us, and “try harder” is how you lose that fight. The numbers aren't flattering to either side of the argument, but they are real: Gloria Mark's research at UC Irvine found average time on a single screen fell from about 2½ minutes in 2004 to roughly 47 seconds, and that it takes over twenty minutes to properly return to a task after an interruption.

Takeaway: Use the model on what you control — your hours, your settings, your team's norms. Don't let it talk you into blaming yourself for a system built to interrupt you.
★
At Mozaik: Which means: if a tool genuinely makes the work impossible, saying so is a legitimate answer. Changing the tool is allowed. It just isn't the first thing to try.
The one thing to remember

You can't call it a distraction until you've decided what you were doing instead.

The model runs in order. Handle the feeling, plan the time, cut the triggers, then lock the door. Skip to step three and all you get is a quieter phone and the same restless hour.
